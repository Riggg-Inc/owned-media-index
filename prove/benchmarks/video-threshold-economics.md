# Video Threshold Economics

## What It Is

A decision rubric for evaluating video podcast investment as a customer-acquisition and monetization decision rather than a reflexive response to platform pressure. Every show has a "Video Threshold": the point at which incremental reach and customer value cover the added production cost of shooting, editing, and distributing video.

The pattern gives teams a repeatable way to answer "when should we invest in video production?" using monetization model, audience lifetime value, production lift, channel acquisition cost, and break-even volume — before committing to a full video workflow.

## Best For

- Owned media programs deciding whether to add video to an audio-first show.
- B2B and pipeline-driven programs where video cost must map to real customer or revenue value.
- Teams under platform pressure (YouTube, Spotify, Instagram) to "go video" without a business case.
- Producers building a budget rationale for or against a video production upgrade.
- Programs re-evaluating an existing video investment that is not paying back.

## Why It Works

Video production materially increases cost, and most shows do not clear the threshold where that cost is recovered. Trade analysis of the Cost of Attention data models average monthly production cost rising from roughly $388 (audio-only) to $1,267 (audio + video), per-episode cost rising from about $67 to $244, and cost per listener-hour rising from roughly $0.56 to $0.99. On a programmatic-only monetization model, break-even shifts from roughly 12,000 downloads/episode (audio) to roughly 50,000 video plays/episode (video).

The same analysis notes that only about 10 of the top 200 US podcasts offer video (per cited Bloomberg reporting), meaning the overwhelming majority of even top shows remain audio-first despite platform incentives. Treating video as an investment decision — gated by a break-even threshold and the show's actual monetization model — keeps teams from adding a large recurring cost that its audience size and revenue model cannot fund.

The rubric also generalizes beyond programmatic revenue: for owned media where the value is pipeline, sponsorship, or brand, the "return" side of the threshold is customer LTV or deal value, not ad impressions — which can lower the effective break-even substantially.

## Required Elements

- Monetization model identified (programmatic, sponsorship, subscription, pipeline/CAC, brand).
- Current audience volume per episode (downloads and, if any, video plays).
- Baseline audio-only production cost per episode and per month.
- Projected audio + video production cost per episode and per month.
- Value-per-unit input appropriate to the model (ad CPM, sponsor rate, subscriber LTV, or customer/deal value and CAC).
- Break-even volume calculation on the chosen monetization model.
- Explicit go/no-go threshold and the assumptions behind it.
- Re-evaluation trigger (audience growth, cost change, or monetization shift).

## Quality Bar

The team can state, in one line, the show's break-even volume for video on its actual monetization model, compare it to current volume, and produce a defensible go/no-go decision with named cost and value assumptions. A decision that cannot show the threshold math does not meet the bar.

## When Not To Use

- When video is already required by the customer or format contract and the decision is not open.
- When the show has no monetization or customer-value model at all — resolve that first; the threshold math needs a value side.
- As a hard rule against video for brand or strategic bets that are explicitly not expected to pay back on volume.
- When the cost and value inputs are pure guesses; use it as a framework, not false precision.
